# Apocalypse

## Authorship and Audience

Revelation names its author as "John" on four occasions (1:1, 4, 9; 22:8). The unanimous tradition of the early church attests that this John was the Apostle John, the son of Zebedee and author of the Fourth Gospel and three epistles. Second century testimonies from prominent figures such as Justin Martyr, Irenaeus (who was a disciple of Polycarp, who was a disciple of John himself), Clement of Alexandria and Tertullian confirm the apostolic authorship.
John wrote the book from the island of Patmos, a small rocky island in the Aegean Sea, where he had been exiled by the Roman authorities "because of the word of God and the testimony of Jesus" (1:9). The book was addressed to the seven churches in the Roman province of Asia (present-day western Turkey), offering them encouragement under persecution, warnings against compromise, and assurance of Christ's ultimate triumph.

## Date and Historical Context

The dating of Revelation generally falls into two views: an earlier date during the reign of Nero (c. 64-68 AD) or a later date during the late reign of Domitian (c. 95-96 AD). The traditional and most widely accepted view, strongly supported by Irenaeus and other ancient witnesses, is the date **95-96 CE.** During Domitian's reign, emperor worship became increasingly obligatory, and Christian congregations that refused to worship the emperor faced increasing persecution.
The historical context is one of present suffering and the threat of martyrdom. The churches suffered external pressure from the Roman state (the "beast") and pagan religions (the "false prophetess" and the "prostitute"), in addition to internal pressure from false teachers who promoted cultural assimilation (such as the Nicolaitans). The book serves to pull back the curtain (the literal meaning of *apokalypsis*) to show these churches the heavenly reality behind their earthly suffering: Christ is on the throne and ruling, and the empire that oppresses them is already under divine judgment.

## Theological Themes

- **The Supreme Sovereignty of God and Christ.** The central vision of Revelation is that of God enthroned in heaven (chapter 4) and the Lamb who was slain ruling with Him (chapter 5). Despite the apparent power of evil empires, the throne of the universe is occupied, and it is God who dictates the terms of history. The word "throne" occurs more than 40 times in the book.
- **The Judgment of Evil and the Cost of Discipleship.** The book portrays without mincing words the terrible outpouring of God's wrath on a rebellious world (through seals, trumpets and bowls). However, it also shows that the church must overcome not by military force, but by "the blood of the Lamb and the word of their testimony" (12:11). Martyrdom is not seen as defeat, but as the supreme victory over the beast.
- **The Consummation of Redemption.** Revelation serves as the literary and theological counterpoint to Genesis. What was ruined by the fall in Genesis is restored and glorified in Revelation. The sea disappears, death is defeated, the curse is removed, the tree of life is returned to humanity, and God begins to physically dwell among His people in the New Jerusalem.
- **The False Triumvirate versus the Trinity.** A fascinating structure of the book is the false satanic trinity: the Dragon (false god), the Beast from the sea (false Christ, who has a false mortal wound healed) and the Beast from the earth (false holy spirit, who directs worship to the first beast). The book exposes the imitative and ultimately futile nature of evil.

## Outline

**I. The Things You Saw [1]**
> A. The Prologue [1:1–8]
> B. The Vision of the Glorified Christ [1:9–20]

**II. The Things That Are (The Seven Churches) [2 – 3]**
> A. Ephesus: The Loveless Church [2:1–7]
> B. Smyrna: The Persecuted Church [2:8–11]
> C. Pergamum: The Tolerant Church [2:12–17]
> D. Thyatira: The Compromising Church [2:18–29]
> E. Sardis: The Dead Church [3:1–6]
> F. Philadelphia: The Faithful Church [3:7–13]
> G. Laodicea: The Tepid Church [3:14–22]

**III. The Things That Will Happen After These [4 – 22]**
> A. Worship on the Celestial Throne [4 – 5]
> B. The Seven Seals of Judgment [6 – 8:1]
> C. The Seven Trumpets of Judgment [8:2 – 11:19]
> D. The Cosmic Conflict: The Woman, the Dragon and the Beasts [12 – 13]
> E. Interlude Visions of Grace and Judgment [14 – 15]
> F. The Seven Bowls of Wrath [16]
> G. The Fall and Judgment of Babylon [17 – 18]
> H. The Triumphal Return of Christ [19]
> I. The Millennium and the Final Judgment [20]
> J. New Heavens, New Earth and the New Jerusalem [21 – 22:5]
> K. The Epilogue: Promise of Soon Return [22:6–21]

## Observations

- The book of Revelation is unique in the New Testament in being the only work devoted entirely to prophecy (1:3). It is the book of consummation, interweaving prophetic threads that began in Genesis and run through the Scriptures. It features hundreds of allusions to the Old Testament, especially to Ezekiel, Daniel, Zechariah, and Isaiah, although it never quotes the Old Testament directly.
- The title of the book in Greek is *Apokalypsis*, which means "an unveiling" or "a revelation". It is not intended to be a confusing puzzle, but rather the lifting of a veil to reveal the invisible reality that Jesus Christ is the supreme Lord and King.
- The book contains seven beatitudes (blessings) for believers: 1:3; 14:13; 16:15; 19:9; 20:6; 22:7; and 22:14.
- One of the most captivating images in the book is the lion, which is actually a Lamb. When John weeps because no one is worthy to open the seals, he is told, "Do not weep; behold, the Lion of the tribe of Judah... has overcome." However, when he turns to see the Lion, he sees "a Lamb, as if it had been slain" (5:5-6). Christ's cosmic victory is achieved through His sacrificial suffering.
