# Jeremiah

## Authorship and Audience

According to Jeremiah 1:1, the book records "the words of Jeremiah the son of Hilkiah of the priests who were in Anathoth in the land of Benjamin." Jeremiah himself is widely accepted as the primary author, with the scribe Baruch, son of Neriah, serving as his secretary and likely editor (see Jeremiah 36:4, 45:1). Baruch's role was significant: at Jeremiah's dictation, he wrote the prophecies on a scroll (36:4), read them publicly (36:10), and probably compiled and organized the book after Jeremiah's death. The book was written primarily for Judah (the Southern Kingdom), although it also contains oracles against neighboring nations (chapters 46–51) and addresses the exiles in Babylon (chapters 29–31).

## Date and Historical Context

Jeremiah 1:2–3 places the beginning of Jeremiah's ministry in "the thirteenth year of the reign of Josiah" (c. **627 B.C.**) and its conclusion "until the eleventh year of Zedekiah" (c. **586 B.C.**), when Jerusalem fell to Babylon. His ministry lasted about **forty years** through the reigns of Josiah, Jehoahaz, Jehoiakim, Jehoiakim, and Zedekiah (1:2–3). After the fall of Jerusalem, Jeremiah was taken to Egypt against his will by a remnant of Jews who fled there (43:6–7), where he probably died.
This was one of the most catastrophic periods in Israel's history. Assyria, which had destroyed the Northern Kingdom in 722 BC, was crumbling; the Neo-Babylonian Empire was on the rise under Nebuchadnezzar; and Egypt competed for regional dominance. Judah became a vassal state caught between these powers, and its leadership repeatedly made foolish political and spiritual alliances rather than trusting in God. Jeremiah preached throughout this period, calling Judah to repentance before the inevitable judgment arrived.

## Theological Themes

- **God's faithfulness to the covenant despite Israel's unfaithfulness.** Jeremiah's fundamental message is rooted in the Mosaic covenant. Israel broke it repeatedly (11:10), and the judgment is therefore not arbitrary but guaranteed by the covenant. However, God's covenant faithfulness is also the very basis of hope: because God is faithful, He will not permanently abandon His people (31:3, 33:19–26).
- **True versus false prophecy.** One of the most pressing pastoral concerns in Jeremiah is the prevalence of false prophets who proclaimed "Peace, peace" when there was no peace (6:14, 8:11). Jeremiah's confrontation with Hananiah (28) is the paradigmatic case. The distinguishing marks of a true prophet, as Jeremiah presents them, are: speaking in the name of Yahweh, calling people to repentance, and having his words fulfilled (28:9; Deuteronomy 18:20–22).
- **Individual responsibility and inner transformation.** Jeremiah pushes the prophetic tradition toward a more inner understanding of covenant faithfulness. The famous "New Covenant" passage (31:31–34) promises that the law will be written on the heart rather than on tablets of stone. This does not abandon the communal dimension of the covenant, but adds a depth of individual internalization that paves the way for the New Testament teaching on regeneration (John 3:3–8; 2 Corinthians 3:3).
- **God's sovereignty over the nations.** Jeremiah's calling included authority "over nations and over kingdoms" (1:10). The oracles against the nations (46–51) demonstrate that Yahweh is not merely a tribal deity but the God who raises and topples empires. Nebuchadnezzar is even called "my servant" (25:9; 27:6)—a shocking designation that underscores God's sovereign use of even pagan rulers to accomplish His purposes.
- **The cost of faithfulness.** Jeremiah is the most autobiographically transparent of all the prophets. His "confessions" (11:18–12:6; 15:10–21; 17:14–18; 18:19–23; 20:7–18) reveal a man in genuine anguish regarding his calling. He was forbidden to marry (16:1–4), rejected by his own family (12:6), imprisoned and thrown into a cistern (38:6). His suffering prefigures the suffering of Christ, who also wept over Jerusalem (Luke 19:41) and was rejected by His own people.

## Observations

- Jeremiah is quoted or has allusions made to him more than fifty times in the New Testament. Notably, more than half of these references appear in the book of Revelation, where the fall of Babylon in Revelation 17–18 draws extensively on Jeremiah 50–51.
- The geographic origin of Jeremiah — **Anathoth in Benjamin** — is significant. Anathoth was the priestly city to which Abiathar had been exiled by Solomon (1 Kings 2:26). Jeremiah, therefore, comes from a lineage of priests associated with the Ark of the Covenant and a priestly tradition that had already suffered rejection.
- Jeremiah 31:15, "Rachel weeping for her children," is quoted in Matthew 2:18 in connection with Herod's massacre of the innocents, illustrating how New Testament authors read Jeremiah's oracles as having multiple layers of fulfillment.
- The structure of the book is deliberately non-chronological. Chapters 26 and 36, for example, describe events in the reign of Jehoiakim, but are placed after chapters pertaining to the reign of Zedekiah. This suggests that Baruch organized the material thematically rather than chronologically.
- Jeremiah's purchase of a field in Anathoth while the Babylonian army was actively besieging Jerusalem (32:1–15) is one of the most striking acted parables in Scripture. Buying land during a siege was commercially irrational—which is precisely the point. It was a dramatic prophecy promulgated of restoration.
